# Speaking in Tongues

I wish to put forward the thesis that speaking a dialect of your own language,
which is not native to you should not really be regardless any different than
speaking a language, other than your first one. This issue called for my
attention after I heard some misguided criticism of Congresswoman Jasmine
Crockett, who represents the 30th district of Texas.

Crockett has at times been criticized for "code switching" into
"black English," or, as it is formally known, African-American Vernacular
English (henceforth AAVE)
when she is addressing an audience of AAVE speakers,
despite the fact that she was educated at elite schools. (See, for instance:
https://www.dailywire.com/news/her-code-switch-is-broken-jasmine-crockett-mocked-over-dramatic-change-in-speech.)
While I am not familiar with her policy stances, and have no idea
if I would like them if I were, these criticisms of her are, as mentioned,
unfounded. And discussing why that is so will, I think, lead us into an
interesting exploration of the relationship between "dialects" and "standard"
forms of a language. 

Let us first ask, "Why shouldn't someone, in some circumstances, speak a dialect
of their language that is not the one they were raised in?" Often, attempts to
do so can result in accusations of "appropriation," or an assumption that the
non-native person speaking the dialect is mocking its native speakers 

However, when I have fumbled along speaking Italian in Genoa, or French in
Martinique, or German in Interlaken, or Hindi in Chandigar, or Hungarian in
Budapest, no one I tried speaking with has ever been angry at me for appropriating
their language, or accused me of mocking it. Instead, everyone seemed to
appreciate the fact that I was trying to speak to them in their own language,
rather than simply talking at them in louder and louder English. This was
especially true of the people of Budapest, who always seemed stunned that a
visitor was even attempting to speak Hungarian. (One cab driver I rode with
actually told me I was the first tourist he had ever met who had spoken to him
in Hungarian.)

So if my rudimentary attempts at speaking these languages were acceptable
behavior, why would it be the case that if I speak African American vernacular
English (henceforth, AAVE) during a conversation, a dialect which I know much
better than any of the above languages, I would be guilty of some sort of
ideological faux pas? 

Crockett speaks AAVE more naturally than George Bush speaks Spanish. (Bush got
all the text right whenever I have heard him, but he always sounded like he was
speaking English, but using only Spanish loanwords.) And yet I don't see there
is anything wrong at all with Bush speaking Spanish when he did.


Someone may try to differentiate these cases by claiming that while
Spanish is a real language and is grammatical, AAVE is neither: it is just an
ungrammatical way of speaking standard English. But there is simply no
justification in linguistic science for such a claim.



There are no native dialects that do not have their own grammatical rules as
regular as those of any standard language. (Pidgin languages,
which are highly simplified grammatically and limited in vocabulary, typically
arise as a way of facilitating trade between two groups who speak very different
languages. A pidgin is neither group's native tongue, and the simplification
allows for effective communication, in a very limited context, to be established
very quickly.) In general, what is going on when someone claims that a person
speaking their native dialect is speaking a language “ungrammatically" is that
through schooling and/or prejudice, they believe that only their own way of
speaking that language is “grammatical.” 

(John McWhorter???)

If a speaker of AAVE says, for instance, "He nuts!"
many people will say, "That is ungrammatical! They left out the verb 'is' in
that sentence!”

But, in Hungarian it is common to skip the copula  (a form of the verb 'to be')
in sentences, and only
include the subject and object. Are all Hungarian speakers speaking Hungarian
ungrammatically? If they are not, then why are speakers of AAVE being
ungrammatical when they do the same thing? Yes, speakers of "Ivy League English"
do not do that, but so speakers of many European languages other than
Hungarian do not regularly leave the copula out of their sentences. Different
languages have different rules, but so do different dialects of one language. If
we do not criticize Hungarian as being "wrong" because its grammar is different
than that of Spanish, why do we find the need to criticize AAVE? Because its
grammar is different from that of Ivy League English?

And when a speaker of AAVE says, "He be fool," many people will say, "That is
also ungrammatical! They used the wrong form of the verb 'to be' in that
sentence!" But, in fact, "He be fool" is a perfectly grammatical sentence in
AAVE, and it means something different than "He is a fool." The former means
that he is a fool in general, while the latter means that he is a fool at this
particular moment.

AAVE is as grammatical a language as "Ivy League English."
Anyone moderately familiar with AAVE will know that "he be fool” is a
grammatically correct sentence in that dialect, while "he are fool" and “he am
fool" are grammatically incorrect. 
"He be fool" is a case of the "invariant be"; see:

https://ygdp.yale.edu/phenomena/invariant-be

None of this means that "standard" English should not be taught in schools, any
more than the fact that Sicilian, Calabrese and Piedmontese are perfectly
grammatical ways of speaking means that standard Italian should not be taught
throughout that country. It is useful for the people in any nation to have a
common tongue in which matters of common importance can be discussed. But there
is no need to denigrate other ways of speaking present in that country in order
to appreciate the importance of a national standard.

Of course these nonstandard ways of speaking have often been denigrated,
for example, in new-born countries (such as Italy and Germany in the nineteenth
century) that were just forming a national identity. Doing so must have seemed
like an expedient way to speed that process up: telling people, "It's good for
us to have a common national tongue: please study this one," may not be as
immediately effective as telling people, "The way you are speaking is stupid and
wrong." But the former message, at least, has the advantage of being truthful,
and, in fact, may, in the long run, actually produce better national cohesion. 

Consider that, today, with a large influx of Spanish speakers into the U.S.,
no one suggests educating them in English requires telling them that Spanish is
just a really, really bad form of English. When President Kennedy spoke German
in Berlin, or President Bush spoke Spanish when addressing a Hispanic audience,
I never saw anyone claiming that they were "talking down" to their audience or
pandering to them. 

And Crockett speaks AAVE more naturally than George Bush speaks Spanish. (Bush got
all the text right whenever I have heard him, but he always sounds like he is
speaking English just with all Spanish loanwords.) And yet I don't see there is
anything wrong at all with Bush speaking Spanish when he did.

What if she went to France to give a talk, and spoke to the people there in
French? Would these critics of hers complain that she didn't grow up speaking
French?


In my own case,
African-American vernacular English (AAVE) is a dialect I've been hearing my
entire life, and after four years of playing music regularly in the black gospel
milieu of the South Carolina Midlands, it is a dialect I know very well. Just as
sometimes I might use the Tagalog phrase “Aray ko” if I suffer a minor hurt, or
say “uno di piu” if I want another beer, or tell my wife “Vite vite” if we must
hurry to get somewhere, I equally might say, “Man, you ain’t never gonna get
nowhere with that” if I am very skeptical about someone's chances of advancing
some project. (One thing that AAVE provides the speaker here is the ability
to express increasing skepticism by piling up negatives: “ain’t,” “never,” and
“nowhere” work together to express the near impossibility of what the person
addressed wants to achieve.

In fact, having heard AAVE my entire life, and being immersed in it at least
once a week for the last four years, speaking AAVE is significantly more natural
to me than speaking Tagalog, or Italian, or French. So if it is OK for me to
sometimes speak in one of those languages, why would it be wrong for me to
sometimes speak in AAVE?

Or, to return to the case, the prompted this essay, why is it wrong for Jasmine
Crockett to sometimes speak in AAVE?

We pick up speech patterns from our milieu. And when our milieu often includes
speakers of a dialect different from our own, we naturally learn their
patterns, and make use of them when they suit our purposes.


Much like we would treat or tourist, who was kind of OK with our language, but
was obviously not a native speaker: we would not dream of heeping abuse on him
for mocking our language, because he asked us where the museum is in English
instead of Mongolian.


If I am forbidden from speaking AAVE because I am not a native speaker of that
dialect, why am I not forbidden from speaking French or Spanish or German or
Italian, because I am not a native speaker of any of those languages? I've
conversed with native speakers in all four, and they all seemed happy to help me
out when I was lost and pleased that I was making an effort rather than just
assuming that they could speak English.

(The one time I did have a problem with a native speaker was when trying to
speak French to a cab driver in Paris, who repeatedly refused to understand what
I was saying. But when I told my friends in Strasbourg about this the next day,
they said "don't worry, the Parisians treat us the same way.")

Black children, and indeed any child who natively speaks any dialect of English
other than standard English, should be told, "The way you grew up speaking is
perfectly fine. It is just as grammatical as any other language. You didn't grow
up making a whole bunch of mistakes when you were talking. However, there is
another dialect of English which is standardly spoken by corporate management,
high-level government employees, and in national media, that you should learn to
speak if you want to participate in any of those arenas."

This is the right way to address this issue, because it is true, it avoids the
idiocy of telling native speakers of a dialect that they are speaking it
incorrectly, and avoids making speakers of non-broadcast dialect feel inferior
to those who grew up speaking more in line with broadcast English

A final reason that some people might object to someone speaking a dialect other
than their native one, that is also sometimes used to complain about people who
speak a foreign language with "too much" of the native accent of that language, is:
"That way you are speaking," they say, "it's just not *you*."

But this confuses the substance of the person with an accidental property of
the person.
It is an accident of my birth that I speak American English. If I had
been kidnapped by Yemeni pirates when I was a few months old and then raised in
Yemen, I would still be me, but my native tongue would be Yemeni Arabic.
Since this is an accidental property of the person, learning a very different
way of expressing themselves in speech is in no way "inauthentic": I can speak
Italian as "authentically" (though much less capably) than I can speak English.
Of course, such accidents play an important role in how our lives proceed. But
you would still be you, however many of such accidents you changed, just you
with a different tool kit to handle a different set of circumstances.
