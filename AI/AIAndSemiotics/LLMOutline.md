## What is the essence of a computer?

### The Universal Turing Machine

In the late 1930s, Alan Turing described a remarkably simple theoretical machine—a tape, a read-write head, and a set of rules—that could read, write, and move along the tape. Despite its simplicity, this model could instantiate any algorithm.

Another UTM
Stephen Wolfram created the "Rule 110" cellular automaton and suspected it was Turing complete. In 2004, Matthew Cook proved that it is.

### The Lambda Calculus

Can simulate any Turing machine.
And vice-versa.

### The Church–Turing Thesis

All UTM equivalents can run the same set of programs.
No hardware upgrade expands what can be computed. Only how fast.

### How Does a Digital Computer "Compute"?

Modern computers are possible because any system with two reliable states --
like on/off or hot/cold -- can perform computation. 

We use electronics not because they're theoretically superior, but because
they're far faster, even though a water or pneumatic computer could compute the
same things. 

To de-mystify computation, let's build a water computer.

All Binary Input Is Similarly Ambiguous

Modern LLMs may seem fundamentally different from a water computer, but similar
claims were made for earlier technologies, and all those claims overlook the
Church–Turing Thesis!

In principle, any system capable of computation can perform the same
calculations, and LLM training itself begins by stripping inputs of meaning into
abstract representations.

If an LLM "learns" to connect badgers and burrows, it really has just learned to connect one pattern of 0s and 1s with another such pattern.

### But Don't LLMs Change All This?

An LLM is a very large and complex computer, but it is still a computer. It is
still a Turing machine, and it is still subject to the Church–Turing Thesis.

Modern LLMs have upwards of a trillion parameters, but they are still just
running a function that takes a string of 0s and 1s as input and produces
another string of 0s and 1s as output. The fact that the function is very
complex does not change the fact that it is still just a function.

### But Isn't That What Human Brains Do?

That is not an argument for LLMs knowing the external world:
It is an argument that humans do not know it!
(And, in fact, our brains are very different from Turing machines.)

Embodied cognition is one explanation for why humans can know the world, but it
is not an argument that LLMs can.

### Secondness

Peirce developed the concept of secondness.

When a rock tumbling down a hill meets another falling rock and deflects its course, we have a purely dyadic relationship: neither rock means anything further when hitting its companion.

### Thirdness

Peirce pointed out that human language, on the other hand, is intrinsically a triadic relationship.

If I shout to you "A mind-reductionist is creeping up behind you!" I am not
merely sending some sound waves to your eardrums: I am also referring to a third
