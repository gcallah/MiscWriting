# Truth or consequences? 

I had a neighbor who used his leaf blower, I imagine, more than any other human
being in history. Knowing that those infernal devices are a source of tremendous
noise and air pollution, and believing that he could easily have used a rake
instead, I decided to shoot him. It seemed obvious to me that the overall
consequences of discouraging the use of such contraptions would easily outweigh
the value of his life... He was an old fellow, after all, and wasn't even
contributing to GDP!

The above is just a fantasy, of course, although I did occasionally consider
shooting the leaf blower itself, especially when he was using it at 7:00 a.m. on
a Sunday morning. But I share that fantasy as an example of consequentialist
moral reasoning. The subtitle of this website is "Place. Limits. Liberty." so it
is worth addressing consequentialism here because it runs roughshod over the
first two, and can easily lead to the destruction of the third.

The breakdown of traditional European society, triggered largely by the print
revolution (see Mary Harrington's forthcoming book, *The King and the Swarm*,
for more on this), and the subsequent rationalism kicked off first and foremost
by Bacon and Descartes, prompted a rather desperate, four-century-long effort to
reground morality on a "rational" basis. One of the less tasty fruits of that
effort is consequentialism, and its subcategory, utilitarianism.
(Consequentialism says we should judge actions solely by the consequences they
produce, without specifying which aspect of those consequences we should be
focused on. Utilitarianism says it is the utility, or pleasure, produced by
actions that are the consequences that matter.)

By positing one universal solvent for moral dilemmas, consequentialism acts to
erode the particularity of different places. All of a culture's customs and
mores must be evaluated based on their easily discerned consequences. If in a
particular culture, for instance, everyone is required to take off their shoes
before entering a home, this custom will be judged based upon some surface
criterion, like "Is the time spent taking one's shoes on and off worth the
increased cleanliness inside the home?" Whatever answer one comes up with, it
ignores the fact that there may be a deeper meaning attached to the practice:
removing one's outside shoes at the threshold of the home sets apart the world
inside the home as a distinct realm from the world outside. But whatever the
"consequences" of such a distinction may be, it is far too difficult for the
consequentialist to pretend to calculate whether it is "worthwhile" to preserve.
So such deeper meanings will simply be ignored, and the argument pro and con
will turn on the utilitarian issue of keeping the floor clean.

But even more dramatically, consequentialism entails a wholesale rejection of
limits; in particular, our very limited knowledge of what "all" the consequences
of some action we undertake will be. The repercussions of anything I choose to
do can, over time, affect almost anyone on the globe, either negatively or
positively. 

For instance, I stopped at a local bookstore to work on this essay, and ordered
a hot latte. While browsing the shelves, I found a book by two friends, which I
did not know existed, and decided to buy it. Perhaps my increasing the demand
for coffee will help prop up some cruel dictator in a coffee-growing country, or
help fund a revolution leading to his overthrow. as I write this sentence, the
extreme air conditioning in the bookstore is making me shiver: maybe my
trembling hands will cause me to have an accident as I drive home later, and
kill a man who is about to invent the cure for cancer... or kill a man who would
become the 21st-century Adolf Hitler. It could be that, unbeknownst to me, one
of my friends has a gambling problem, and the royalties from the book I bought
will encourage him to gamble even more. Chaos theory famously points out that a
butterfly flapping its wings in Brazil may help produce a tornado in Texas some
time later. Obviously, every human action can have as great, or even greater,
unforeseen consequences.

In contrast to consequentialism, respecting our inherited moral traditions is a
way of acknowledging our own limits: they represent the accumulated wisdom of
generations of our ancestors. It is very unlikely that my own, limited mind can
think up superior morality from scratch. Of course, those traditions may be in
need modification, but those modifications will always make use of other parts
of the tradition in addressing the part that needs to be amendment.
Consequentialism is a way to try to replace tradition all in one go, with
"rational calculation."

Given that no one can actually perform the sort of calculations that genuine
consequentialism would require, what *actually* takes place when some action is
justified based upon its supposed consequences? Australian philosopher John
Finnis describes the usual procedure: 

"Now, individuals and societies do in fact solve these problems for themselves,
and so make [consequentialism] *seem* workable. They focus on something which
they have already set their hearts on [such as]... an end to the war, the
detection of those heretics or criminals, re-election as president, an end to
that young woman's suffering... The good consequences of this, and the bad
consequences of omitting or failing to get it, are dwelt upon... Thus, the
calculus is forced through to provide a determinate solution [that will be] the
quickest, cheapest way of getting what was first focused upon[, for example,]
the nuclear or firestorm bombing of the enemy's hostage civilians, the
inquisitorial torture of suspects or informers, the fraudulent cover-up and
destruction of legal process, the abortion of unborn and 'exposure' of newly
born children... Of course, by focusing on some other alternatives, and on some
other long-term or overall consequences of choosing the favorite alternative,
and on the life possibilities of the proposed victims, and so on, one can in
every case find reasons to condemn the favorite action on 'consequentialist
grounds'. But in truth, both sets of calculations in such cases are equally
senseless. What generates the 'conclusions' is always something other than the
calculus..." (*Natural Law & Natural Rights*, page 117)

In other words, consequentialism is a neat trick that allows you to cook up a
justification for whatever it is you wanted to do anyway. 

Finally, turning to the third member of our website's triumvirate,
consequentialism can endanger liberty as well. There have been thinkers, such as
Ludwig von Mises and Henry Hazlitt, who have used consequentialist reasoning to
reach libertarian conclusions. However, since no one can really predict with
confidence all the outcomes of any policy, much less prove them to everyone's
satisfaction, there are even more people who have used it as an argument to
restrict liberty. For instance, it was an image  of the horrific consequences
that might result from allowing people to make their own health and safety
decisions during COVID that were used to justify lockdowns. (And I'm not even
making any claim about the wisdom of the lockdowns here, just noting that,
whether wise or not, they most certainly restricted people's liberty!)

None of this is to say that we should ignore the foreseeable results of our
choices. If I am thinking of driving the family car down the highway at 120
miles per hour, the high chance of a fatal crash at such a speed should lead me
to rule out that option. To ignore that danger would be a failure to exercise
the virtue of prudence. The problem with consequentialism is not that it urges
us to pay attention to consequences, but rather its claim that the *only* things
we need to pay attention to when acting are consequences.

Prudence should enter into our considerations only when choosing among actions
that are *otherwise* licit. If I begin thinking about what the consequences
would be of killing a relative so that I can inherit his money, I have had, as
the English philosopher Bernard Williams put it, "one thought too many." One can
always imagine some scenario in which the money I inherit would be put to a much
better use than how my relative presently uses it, and so, considering  the
"overall consequences," it can create the illusion that killing him is a good
idea. This is a trope in many detective shows, where some doctor or scientist,
who is about to make a breakthrough medical discovery, kills the person who is
going to report him for fraud because "their life was worth less than the many
lives I'm going to save." That person is rightly seen as a villain.

Oddly enough, many people, who would readily agree on the villainy of the
detective show antagonist, throw that moral intuition out the window when it
comes to the actions of their own government. So we can regularly find people
online claiming that, if the U.S. has to wipe out the entire population of Iran
in order to eliminate the threat of the Iranian government getting nuclear
weapons, so be it.

I think there are two reasons this happens: first of all, many people
understandably do not want to think badly of their own government. If they see
that their government *has* acted based on this sort of consequentialist
reasoning, it is more comfortable to accept it than not. Secondly, the
governmental officials who have justified their actions based upon
consequentialist arguments, or who hope to do so in the future, are anxious to
"educate" the population to accept such reasoning.

Naturally, officials directing a nation's war efforts, even in a fully just war,
will often face difficult and tragic decisions. It may be impossible to strike
at the enemy without a very high chance of injuring or killing non-combatants,
perhaps very many non-combatants. And it won't always be obvious just what
exercising sufficient care for non-combatants will entail, or what military
targets were truly vital to hit, despite probable civilian casualties, versus
which ones actually could have been left alone without much risk. But as murky
and fraught nature as such decisions are, those foggy areas are very benign
weather compared to deliberately targeting civilians in order to bring one's
enemy to its knees. 

As Finnis put it:

"For every government that has the physical capacity to make its threats
credible says this to its potential enemies: 'If you attack us and threaten to
defeat us, we will kill all the hostages we hold; that is to say, we will
incinerate or dismember as many of your old men and women and children, and
poison as many of your mothers and their unborn offspring, as it takes to
persuade you to desist; we do not regard as decisive the fact that they are
themselves no threat to us; no do we propose to destroy them merely
incidentally, as an unsought-after side-effect of efforts to stop your armed
forces in their attack on us; no, we will destroy your noncombatants precisely
because you value them, and in order to *persuade* you to desist." (*Natural Law
& Natural Rights*, page 224)
